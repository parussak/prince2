Key message: projects deliver change to people

The purpose of a project is to deliver change, affecting people’s business-as-usual activities, routines and responsibilities. How well that change is implemented—and therefore how well the project performs—depends on the project team’s capabilities, the strength of relationships and the people affected.

BAU seeks stability, whereas a project disrupts the current way of working. Resistance is normal: people may fear losing control, competence, status, a familiar routine or security. It is not a human defect that can be removed by sending one announcement.

People permeate the method. This is not an extra practice or separate process. Behaviours, culture, capabilities and relationships affect the principles, practices, processes and tailoring of PRINCE2.

SnakeBite: change is more than a finished game

Bartek wants a technically ambitious solution, Tomek protects the retro style, Maya fears players will reject the result, and customer support worries about new duties after launch. The external API supplier has its own priorities and culture.

Zosia knows a schedule will not solve conflicting interests. She must build shared purpose, involve the right people in co-creation, agree ways of working, understand resistance, maintain information flow and prepare the organization to move from current BAU to operating SnakeBite.

Organizational and project ecosystems

Organizational ecosystem
Internal elements—including staff, board, owners and stakeholders—together with external relationships such as customers, partners, suppliers, regulators and competitors.
Project ecosystem
The elements of the business involved in or directly affected by the project, together with associated users and suppliers.
A project crosses organizational boundaries
🌐 Organizational ecosystem
BAU, owners, partners, regulators, competitors, politics and culture.
🐍 SnakeBite ecosystem
Project team, users, API supplier, marketing, support and people affected by change.
Shared relationships, decisions, knowledge and change impact

Organizations are changing networks of relationships. Projects change organizations even when this is not their primary purpose. Decisions should be made close to where relevant knowledge resides and escalated when wider project, organizational or political impacts must be considered.

Leading successful change

The means by which an organization transitions from the current state to the target state.

Identifying affected organizational areas describes the change the project will deliver. Where transition is not immediate, interim states should also be described.

The change management approach supports decisions about:

Impact and relationshipsWhich ecosystem areas are affected and which relationships matter?
Capabilities and cultureWhich skills are needed during and after transition, and how will culture evolve?
TransitionLearning and upskilling, transfer of project knowledge or recruitment.

Its level of detail should reflect the decisions it supports, not a desire to create paperwork.

SnakeBite: a finished game is not enough. Support needs new procedures, marketing prepares launch, and operations must receive technical knowledge. Zosia plans these transitions rather than assuming they happen automatically.

Stakeholders and culture

Stakeholder
Any individual, group or organization that can affect, be affected by, or perceive itself to be affected by the project.

Stakeholders include formal project roles and people without a role who are affected by or critical to success. They span business, user and supplier communities and may be outside both the project team and the business.

Culture
The shared attitudes, values, goals and ways of working that characterize a group of people.

A shared understanding developed with key influencers is captured in the project brief. It informs ways of working recorded in management approaches. Role appointments—especially project board decision-makers representing business, user and supplier interests—also sustain that shared understanding.

Leadership, management, collaboration and co-creation

Three complementary capabilities
Leadership
motivating, influencing, persuading and creating shared purpose
Management
instructing task execution in line with agreed ways of working
Communication
connecting, listening, feedback and maintaining alignment
CollaborationPeople across the project ecosystem work together to achieve project objectives.
Co-creationUsers and key influencers help design products and ways of working so they are adopted.
LeadershipWorks through relationships, regular feedback, influence and jointly agreed ways of working.

Co-creating ways of working increases people’s willingness to be managed in line with them. A project manager has formal authority within tolerances, but the actual power structure may differ.

  • Projects are temporary and people often have multiple reporting lines.
  • PRINCE2 roles may not align with job titles.
  • Team members have competing priorities and may be more senior than the project manager.
  • Some motivators, such as pay and career progression, are outside project-manager control.
SnakeBite: Zosia cannot order Bartek and Tomek to trust one another. She can align their interests around the product, involve them in co-creating criteria and use influence and relationships rather than title alone.

Building an effective team

The team needs diverse capabilities, competencies and perspectives matching project requirements. PRINCE2 supports this through:

Project planExplains team goals and creates clear purpose.
Team structure and role descriptionsClarify responsibilities, relationships, coaching and training needs.
Communication management approachExplains engagement, mutual support and relationship development.
PIDRecords agreed ways of working and keeps focus on outcomes.
Product-based planningBuilds agreement on user needs, products, dependencies and sequence.

Psychological safety

Team members should feel accepted and respected and able to express diverse views without fear of negative consequences. Everyone helps create this through daily interactions and care for wellbeing. Teams may need room to experiment, inspect, adapt, self-organize and adjust ways of working.

Trap: psychological safety does not mean weak standards or avoiding difficult conversations. It means people can raise a concern, error or dissenting view without interpersonal punishment.

Bringing the team together and social cohesion

Whether work is co-located, remote or hybrid, collaboration should optimize time and build social cohesion. It grows through purposeful regular contact, trust across silos and deliberate maintenance of relationships that provide resilience when difficulties arise.

🏢 Co-located

Relationships also form organically through desk conversations, kitchens, corridors, shared lunches and social events.

🌐 Remote

Allow unstructured online conversation, listening time, virtual away days and effective collaboration tools.

🔀 Hybrid

Synchronize co-location days for key people and prevent location from creating more- and less-informed groups.

Communication: flow, feedback and resistance

Most organizational communication occurs outside formal channels. Accept this reality: provide information in multiple shareable formats and several pathways for feedback.

Working modeCommunication focus
Co-locatedMonitor formal and informal flow and reconnect isolated people or groups.
RemoteUse deliberate structure, time for unstructured listening and clarity about how information travels through relationships.
HybridPrevent separate groups becoming more or less involved because of location.

Periodic reporting alone is insufficient: people may unconsciously shape information around what seems acceptable, expected or desirable. Openness reduces bias, but projects still need additional feedback routes, strong influencer relationships and monitoring of relationship health.

Resistance is data: pockets of resistance show where listening and communication should focus. They may reveal misconceptions or genuine issues requiring action.

People, practices and processes

The organizing practice explains how people form a temporary project management team, represent the three interests and design the project organization. Seven processes operate through four layers: business, directing, managing and delivering. Behaviour, culture and relationships explain how people interface across those layers.
